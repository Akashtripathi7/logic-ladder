## module: mt01 | Numbers and the number line
summary: Whole numbers, negatives and zero on a number line; comparing numbers; distance and absolute value.
=== en
### The big idea
Every number has a **place on a line**. Once you can picture that line, comparing numbers, counting steps between them and handling negatives all become "just walking".

### The number line
Draw a long straight line. Put **0** in the middle. Walk right and the numbers grow: 1, 2, 3, 4… Walk left and they shrink below zero: −1, −2, −3…

> **Analogy:** Think of the floors in a tall building with a basement. Floor 0 is the ground floor. Floors 1, 2, 3 are above ground. Floors −1, −2 are the basement levels. Going **up** always means a **bigger** number, even in the basement: −1 is higher than −3.

The numbers we use most in coding are **integers**: whole numbers with no decimal part, like −7, 0, 12 and 1000. In Python their type is `int`.

### Comparing numbers
A number further **right** on the line is **bigger**.
- 7 > 3 because 7 is to the right of 3.
- −1 > −5 because −1 is to the right of −5. (Being in debt by ₹1 is better than being in debt by ₹5.)
- 0 is bigger than every negative number and smaller than every positive one.

| Symbol | Read it as | Example |
|---|---|---|
| `<` | is less than | `2 < 9` |
| `>` | is greater than | `9 > 2` |
| `<=` | is less than or equal to | `4 <= 4` |
| `>=` | is greater than or equal to | `5 >= 1` |
| `==` | is equal to | `3 == 3` |
| `!=` | is not equal to | `3 != 4` |

> **Mistake:** A single `=` **stores** a value (`x = 5`). A double `==` **asks** whether two values are equal (`x == 5`). Mixing them up is one of the most common beginner bugs.

### Distance between two numbers
How many steps from 3 to 8? Walk it: 4, 5, 6, 7, 8. That is **5 steps**, and 8 − 3 = 5.

How many steps from −2 to 3? −1, 0, 1, 2, 3: again **5 steps**, and 3 − (−2) = 5.

Distance is never negative. Walking from 8 back to 3 is still 5 steps. To make sure a difference is never negative, use **absolute value**: it throws away the minus sign.

- `abs(8 - 3)` is 5
- `abs(3 - 8)` is also 5
- `abs(-12)` is 12, `abs(12)` is 12

> **Tip:** "How far apart are a and b?" is always `abs(a - b)`, whichever is bigger.

### In Python
```python
a = -4
b = 7
print(a < b)        # True
print(b - a)        # 11
print(abs(a - b))   # 11
print(max(a, b))    # 7, the one further right
print(min(a, b))    # -4, the one further left
```

### Where this shows up in DSA
- Finding the **closest** value to a target (smallest `abs(x - target)`).
- Sorting: numbers go from left to right on the line.
- Temperatures, stock prices and heights are all points on a number line; "the biggest jump" is the biggest distance.

> **Remember:** Right means bigger. Distance is `abs(a - b)`. `=` stores, `==` compares.

- `abs(3 - 8)` bhi 5
- `abs(-12)` = 12, `abs(12)` = 12

## module: mt02 | The four operations and their order
summary: Adding, subtracting, multiplying and dividing as actions; which one happens first; brackets; shortcuts like +=.
=== en
### The big idea
The four operations are four **actions**: join, take away, repeat, share. When several appear together, maths has fixed **rules about which happens first**, and Python follows the same rules.

### Four actions
| Operation | Action | Picture | Python |
|---|---|---|---|
| Addition | **join** groups | 3 apples + 4 apples = 7 apples | `3 + 4` |
| Subtraction | **take away**, or find the gap | 10 sweets, eat 3, 7 left | `10 - 3` |
| Multiplication | **repeat** a group | 4 bags of 5 marbles = 20 | `4 * 5` |
| Division | **share** equally | 20 marbles for 4 kids = 5 each | `20 / 4` |

> **Analogy:** Multiplication is fast addition. 4 × 5 means "5 + 5 + 5 + 5". Imagine 4 rows of 5 chairs: count them one by one, or multiply once.

Python uses `*` for multiply (there is no × key on the keyboard) and `/` for divide.

### Which one goes first?
What is 2 + 3 × 4? If you go left to right you get 5 × 4 = 20. But the rule says **multiply and divide before add and subtract**, so it is 2 + 12 = **14**.

The order, from first to last:
1. **Brackets** `( )`
2. **Powers** `**` (you will meet these in topic 7)
3. **Multiply and divide** `*  /  //  %`, left to right
4. **Add and subtract** `+  -`, left to right

> **Tip:** When in doubt, add brackets. `(2 + 3) * 4` is 20, and anyone reading it knows exactly what you meant. Brackets cost nothing.

Worked example: `10 - 2 * 3 + 8 / 4`
- Multiply first: 2 * 3 = 6 → `10 - 6 + 8 / 4`
- Divide: 8 / 4 = 2.0 → `10 - 6 + 2.0`
- Left to right: 10 − 6 = 4, 4 + 2.0 = **6.0**

Why 6.0 and not 6? Because `/` always gives a decimal number (a `float`) in Python. Topic 3 shows how to get whole-number answers.

### Updating a value
In code we often change a number using its old value:
```python
score = 10
score = score + 5   # score is now 15
score += 5          # the same thing, shorter: now 20
score -= 3          # 17
score *= 2          # 34
```
Read `score += 5` as "score grows by 5". The right side is worked out first, using the **old** value, then stored.

### Where this shows up in DSA
- Every formula in a solution follows these rules: `mid = lo + (hi - lo) // 2` works only because the subtraction is inside brackets.
- Running totals (`total += x`) are the heart of countless loops.

> **Remember:** Brackets, then powers, then × ÷, then + −. `/` gives a decimal. `x += 1` means "x becomes one bigger".

## module: mt03 | Division with remainders
summary: Sharing equally, what is left over, whole-number division with //, the remainder with %, and how they fit together.
=== en
### The big idea
When you share things that cannot be cut, some may be **left over**. Division gives two answers: **how many each person gets** and **how many are left**. Python has one operator for each: `//` and `%`.

### Sharing sweets
You have **14 sweets** and **4 friends**. You hand them out one round at a time: each friend gets 1, then 2, then 3. After 3 rounds you have used 12 sweets. Only 2 remain, not enough for another full round.

- Each friend gets **3**. This is the **quotient**.
- **2** are left over. This is the **remainder**.

| Question | Maths | Python | Answer |
|---|---|---|---|
| How many each? | 14 ÷ 4, whole part | `14 // 4` | 3 |
| How many left? | remainder of 14 ÷ 4 | `14 % 4` | 2 |
| Exact share | 14 ÷ 4 | `14 / 4` | 3.5 |

> **Analogy:** `//` is "how many full boxes can I fill?" and `%` is "how many items are loose?". Packing 23 eggs into boxes of 6: `23 // 6` is 3 full boxes, `23 % 6` is 5 loose eggs.

### The check that always works
The quotient and remainder fit back together:

**number = divisor × quotient + remainder**

14 = 4 × 3 + 2. 23 = 6 × 3 + 5. If this does not add up, you made a mistake.

The remainder is always **smaller than the divisor**. When dividing by 4, the remainder can only be 0, 1, 2 or 3. If you had 4 left over, you could give everyone one more.

### When nothing is left
`12 % 4` is 0: 12 sweets share perfectly among 4 friends. A remainder of **0** means "divides exactly". This is how code checks divisibility:
```python
if n % 4 == 0:
    print("n is a multiple of 4")
```

### Two special cases
- **Dividing a smaller number:** `3 // 5` is 0 (nobody gets a whole sweet) and `3 % 5` is 3 (all 3 are left).
- **Dividing by zero** is impossible: sharing among 0 friends makes no sense. Python stops with `ZeroDivisionError`.

> **Mistake:** In Python, `//` rounds **down** towards the smaller number. For negatives this surprises people: `-7 // 2` is −4, not −3. For the problems in this course you will almost always use positive numbers, so just remember it exists.

### Rounding up
Sometimes you need to round **up**: 14 people, cars that seat 4. `14 // 4` is 3 cars, but 2 people are left on the road. You need **4 cars**. A neat trick for positive numbers:

`(a + b - 1) // b` gives a ÷ b rounded up. (14 + 3) // 4 = 17 // 4 = **4**.

### Where this shows up in DSA
- `% 2` for even and odd, `% k` for "every k-th", `// 2` for halving in binary search.
- Rounding up: Koko Eating Bananas, pages per day, trips needed.

> **Remember:** `//` = how many whole groups. `%` = how many left. number = divisor × quotient + remainder.

## module: mt04 | Modulo: even, odd, clocks and cycles
summary: The remainder as a tool: even and odd, divisibility, wrapping around like a clock, repeating patterns and FizzBuzz.
=== en
### The big idea
`%` gives the remainder, and remainders **go round in circles**. That makes `%` the tool for anything that repeats: even and odd, days of the week, turns in a game, positions that wrap around.

### Even and odd
A number is **even** if it splits into two equal groups with nothing left over. So `n % 2` is 0 for even numbers and 1 for odd ones.

| n | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| n % 2 | 0 | 1 | 0 | 1 | 0 | 1 | 0 | 1 |

The remainders go 0, 1, 0, 1… forever. That repeating pattern is the whole secret of modulo.

### The clock
A clock shows 1 to 12, then starts again. If it is 10 o'clock now, what time is it 5 hours later? Count on: 11, 12, 1, 2, **3**. Not 15.

> **Analogy:** Modulo is a clock with n positions, numbered 0 to n − 1. Any number, however large, lands somewhere on that clock. `n % 5` is "where do I land on a 5-hour clock?".

Days of the week work the same way. If today is day 2 (Tuesday, counting Sunday as 0), what day is it 100 days later? `(2 + 100) % 7` = 102 % 7 = **4**, a Thursday. No calendar needed.

### Divisibility
"Is n divisible by k?" is exactly `n % k == 0`.
- `36 % 4 == 0`: yes, 36 is a multiple of 4.
- `37 % 4 == 1`: no, one left over.

**FizzBuzz**, a classic warm-up: for numbers 1 to 15, print "Fizz" for multiples of 3, "Buzz" for multiples of 5, and "FizzBuzz" for multiples of both.
```python
for n in range(1, 16):
    if n % 15 == 0:
        print("FizzBuzz")
    elif n % 3 == 0:
        print("Fizz")
    elif n % 5 == 0:
        print("Buzz")
    else:
        print(n)
```
> **Tip:** Check the "both" case first. If you check `% 3` first, 15 prints "Fizz" and never reaches "FizzBuzz".

### Wrapping around a list
A list of 5 players takes turns: 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, then back to 0. The player for turn t is `t % 5`. Moving right along a circular list: `(i + 1) % n`. The last position wraps back to 0 automatically.

### Where this shows up in DSA
- Even and odd checks everywhere, "every k-th element", round-robin turns.
- Circular arrays (Gas Station, rotating an array), hashing (`key % size`), cycles in Happy Number.

> **Remember:** `n % 2 == 0` means even. `n % k == 0` means divisible by k. `% n` wraps any number onto a clock of size n.

=== hi
### Bada idea
`%` remainder deta hai, aur remainders **gol-gol ghoomte hain**. Isliye `%` har us cheez ka tool hai jo dohraati hai: even aur odd, hafte ke din, game mein baari, positions jo ghoom ke wapas aati hain.

### Even aur odd
Number **even** hai agar wo do barabar groups mein bina bache bant jaaye. Toh even numbers ke liye `n % 2` = 0 aur odd ke liye 1.

| n | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| n % 2 | 0 | 1 | 0 | 1 | 0 | 1 | 0 | 1 |

Remainders 0, 1, 0, 1… hamesha chalte rehte hain. Yahi dohraata pattern modulo ka poora raaz hai.

### Ghadi
Ghadi 1 se 12 dikhati hai, phir dobara shuru. Abhi 10 baje hain, 5 ghante baad kitne bajenge? Aage gino: 11, 12, 1, 2, **3**. 15 nahi.

> **Analogy:** Modulo n positions wali ghadi hai, 0 se n − 1 tak. Koi bhi number, kitna bhi bada, us ghadi pe kahin na kahin girta hai. `n % 5` matlab "5 ghante wali ghadi pe main kahan girunga?".

Hafte ke din bhi aise hi. Aaj din 2 hai (Tuesday, Sunday ko 0 maano), 100 din baad kaunsa din? `(2 + 100) % 7` = 102 % 7 = **4**, Thursday. Calendar ki zaroorat nahi.

### Divisibility
"Kya n, k se divide hota hai?" bilkul `n % k == 0` hai.
- `36 % 4 == 0`: haan, 36, 4 ka multiple hai.
- `37 % 4 == 1`: nahi, ek bacha.

**FizzBuzz**, ek classic warm-up: 1 se 15 tak, 3 ke multiples pe "Fizz", 5 ke multiples pe "Buzz", aur dono ke multiples pe "FizzBuzz" print karo.
```python
for n in range(1, 16):
    if n % 15 == 0:
        print("FizzBuzz")
    elif n % 3 == 0:
        print("Fizz")
    elif n % 5 == 0:
        print("Buzz")
    else:
        print(n)
```
> **Tip:** "Dono" wala case pehle check karo. Agar `% 3` pehle check kiya, toh 15 "Fizz" print karega aur "FizzBuzz" tak kabhi nahi pahunchega.

### List mein ghoom ke wapas
5 players baari-baari khelte hain: 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, phir wapas 0. Turn t ka player `t % 5` hai. Circular list mein right jaana: `(i + 1) % n`. Aakhri position apne aap 0 pe aa jaati hai.

### DSA mein kahan aata hai
- Har jagah even/odd check, "har k-th element", round-robin baari.
- Circular arrays (Gas Station, array rotate karna), hashing (`key % size`), Happy Number mein cycles.

> **Yaad rakho:** `n % 2 == 0` matlab even. `n % k == 0` matlab k se divisible. `% n` kisi bhi number ko n size ki ghadi pe le aata hai.
